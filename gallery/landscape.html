Terrain as closed band-limited curves. It is the awkward case, and that is the reason for the page: a skyline is a graph rather than a loop, and fractional Brownian motion has no last harmonic — so for the first time in this catalogue the question "how many leaves" has no answer until you say to what tolerance, and the count grows as a power of it rather than a logarithm.

The terrain here is a Fourier series with a power-law envelope and random phases, which is the standard model for topography and also means the spectrum of the ground and the spectrum the format stores are the same object. Every leaf count on this page is therefore computed as well as measured, and the two are printed side by side. The third section is the one to read if you read one: the leaf count turns out to be a ruler in Richardson\'s 1961 sense, and the fractal dimension you get by dividers and the one you get by turning down the rung agree to three decimal places.

Every tile is live: push the dials and the source line under it updates to the call that made what you are looking at. Click the source to copy it.

Under every tile is its spectrum: bar m is how strongly the outline wobbles m times per lap, with m = 0 the round body itself. The scale is logarithmic, so a harmonic that is small but real is a short bar, and one that is exactly zero is a tick on the floor. For a group of loops it shows the largest one.

tvf.landscape.js imports only the public surface of tvf.js — Shape, mix, alignTo, the primitives. Nothing here reaches into the kernel.
Harmonic builders sample uniformly in theta, not arc length, on purpose: arc-length resampling of a spectrally-specified contour is a nonlinear reparameterization, and tb_algebra §10.3 measures the leak at up to 8× the required leaf count. Traced builders take arc length, where there is no spectrum to protect.
